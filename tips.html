Tips & FAQ

Counting calories is a simple skill that gets easier with a few habits. Here's what actually helps — and the answers to the questions people ask most.

Five habits

Make it easier on yourself

1

Weigh before you cook

Rice, pasta and meat change weight as they cook because of water. Weighing raw ingredients and using the raw entry is more accurate — and it's the difference that quietly adds 100–200 kcal to a day.

2

Repeat the same meals

Most people eat a small menu on rotation. If three of your breakfasts and lunches are the same each week, you only ever have to log a handful of meals — accuracy goes up, effort goes down.

3

Log liquids and "bites"

Drinks with sugar and the odd handful of nuts or chocolate are where budgets quietly leak. Not every bite needs weighing, but the ones you add absent-mindedly do — that's usually 200–400 kcal a day hiding in plain sight.

4

Watch the weekly trend, not the daily number

Water, salt and timing move the scale by a kilo or more from one morning to the next. A weekly average over several weeks tells you the truth; any single weigh-in doesn't.

5

Prioritise protein and fibre

They're the two things that make a deficit feel easy. Protein is the most filling macronutrient and protects muscle while you lose; fibre adds volume for very few calories. A 2,000 kcal day of these looks very different from one of chips.

Avoid these

Common mistakes

  • Eyeballing portions. People routinely under-estimate restaurant meals and nuts by half. Weigh at home, and when eating out, over-estimate.
  • Quitting after one bad day. One overshoot doesn't undo a week. Consistency over months is what counts, not perfection on a Tuesday.
  • Cutting too hard. An extreme deficit is miserable, hard to sustain and often ends in a rebound. A moderate 300–500 kcal deficit gets the same result without the crash.
  • Ignoring the trend. Judging progress on a single weigh-in leads to panic over water weight and misses the real direction of the line.

Straight answers

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to count calories forever?
No. For many people it's a temporary learning tool — a few months of logging teaches you what portions actually look like, after which most people can estimate well enough from habit. Use it as training, not a life sentence.
Why is my maintenance number different from a friend's?
Maintenance scales with body size, muscle mass, age, sex and activity. Bigger bodies and more muscle burn more at rest; men typically carry more muscle; and burn rates decline slightly with age. The calculator estimates for you — compare your number only with your own history, not anyone else's.
Is it better to eat small meals more often?
Meal frequency doesn't meaningfully change your metabolism or your daily burn. Eat in whatever pattern keeps you full, satisfied and consistent — that's the pattern you'll stick to, and consistency is what actually works.
Can I eat whatever I want as long as it fits my calories?
In the short term, weight change is mostly about the total. But foods differ in protein, fibre, vitamins and how full they keep you. A budget filled with junk is harder to stay under and leaves you undernourished. "Calories in, macros and quality remembered" is the fuller answer.
Why did my weight loss stall after a few weeks?
A few possibilities: your weight is simply holding water while fat keeps dropping (check the trend), your real maintenance is lower than the estimate because you weigh less now, or your tracking has drifted. Recompute your numbers, tighten the logging, and give it two to three weeks before concluding anything.
Does exercise mean I can eat more?
Yes, but less than people hope. Exercise burns real calories — but a 30-minute run might earn you one pastry, and people often overestimate the burn and eat back more. If you're in a deficit, treat exercise as a bonus deficit rather than a licence to double portions.
